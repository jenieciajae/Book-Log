Book Reviews

Books Review Rating
Men Who Hate Women This was such a good read! It’s both eye-opening and deeply important. Bates goes beyond surface-level discussions of misogyny, using undercover research to expose how online communities like incels, pickup artists, and men’s rights activists actively radicalize men and normalize hatred toward women. Trigger warning: this book discusses misogyny, harassment, and violence against women. I think what makes this book so powerful is how clearly it connects digital misogyny to real-world consequences, from politics to violence, showing that this isn’t just “internet culture,” but a growing social threat. It’s educational without being overwhelming and motivating without being preachy. I genuinely believe more people need to read this, because awareness is the first step in recognizing and challenging these harmful ideologies. 9/10
Book Lovers This one truly lives up to its name — it’s a book for book lovers about book lovers. The story leans into publishing-world charm and literary references in a way that feels self-aware and fun rather than overdone. It’s a light, easy read that still has sharp, thoughtful writing and strong character dynamics. While it has its romantic moments, it never feels overly cheesy, and the chemistry feels grounded instead of forced. Overall, it’s clever, comforting, and perfect if you want something enjoyable that still feels well written. 8.6/10
Notes from Underground This was such a strange but fascinating read. Dostoevsky writes from the perspective of a deeply bitter and self-aware narrator who constantly contradicts himself and questions human nature. The first half feels almost like a rant about free will, rationality, and why people sometimes choose suffering over happiness. The second half shifts into a series of awkward and painful social encounters that really show how isolated and self-destructive the narrator is. It’s not always an easy or comfortable read, but that’s part of what makes it powerful. The book dives deep into insecurity, pride, and the darker parts of the human psyche, and even though it was written in the 1800s it still feels surprisingly modern. 8.8/10
The Bell Jar This book is beautifully written but also incredibly heavy. Sylvia Plath captures the slow and confusing descent into depression in a way that feels painfully honest and personal. The main character, Esther, struggles with expectations about her future, identity, and the pressure to fit into the roles society expects of her. What makes the novel so powerful is how clearly it portrays the feeling of being trapped inside your own mind, like everything around you keeps moving while you feel completely disconnected from it. Even though the subject matter is dark, the writing itself is poetic and thoughtful, and it creates a very intimate look at mental health and identity. 9/10
The Perks of Being a Wallflower This was such an emotional and engaging read, and I ended up finishing it in one sitting. I had already seen the movie beforehand, so I went into the book knowing the general story, but I actually think that helped me appreciate it more. Being familiar with the characters made it easier to focus on Charlie’s perspective and the way the story unfolds through his letters. The writing style is simple but very personal, which makes the emotions feel incredibly real and immediate. One thing worth mentioning is that the book does contain heavy topics, including sexual abuse, so a trigger warning is definitely important for readers to know going in. Despite the difficult subject matter, the story handles these themes with a lot of sensitivity and honesty. Overall, it’s a deeply emotional and relatable book, especially for anyone who has struggled with depression, loneliness, or trying to find their place in the world. 8.5/10
The End of White World Supremacy The book was informative and offered a compelling look into history as well as Malcolm X’s beliefs and perspectives. While I personally do not resonate with his ideas about racial separation or his assertion that every person who is not white is Muslim, I can appreciate the passion and conviction behind his words. His speeches are inspiring and encouraging, reflecting a genuine desire to uplift and unite his community. Malcolm X was clearly a prominent figure in the civil rights movement, and reading this book helped me understand why he had such a significant impact and why his messages resonated so deeply with many people at the time. 6.5/10
The Metamorphosis The book is such a good exploration of identity and what it feels like to be misunderstood, ignored, or dehumanized. Gregor’s experience of being trapped in the same house with his family while not being seen or truly understood is so relatable, especially for young adults and teens navigating family, school, or societal expectations. I love that a book written over 110 years ago can still feel so relevant and resonate with readers today, capturing timeless feelings of isolation, frustration, and the struggle to assert one’s humanity. The story is slightly haunting, yet oddly engaging, and Kafka’s writing makes the unsettling moments feel deeply personal. It was a quick and easy read, but it left a lasting impression, and I would highly recommend it to anyone looking to get into classics or explore thought-provoking literature. I’m already excited to read more of Kafka’s work and see what other insights he has into human nature! 9/10
